The Public Benefit of Improved Trails - What is it actually worth , in dollars?

Hawaiʻi's trails are often valued on a narrow dimension, through standard market valuation, like hiking and camping services. This study takes a wider view, putting a defensible dollar figure on the trail features that usually get overlooked: how much people value a healthier habitat, better-maintained trails, fewer crowds, and what those improvements are worth across both residents and tourists.

What the study measured

We showed people different trail scenarios and asked which they'd prefer.

Rather than asking people directly how much a trail is worth, the survey showed them realistic management options that differed in habitat quality, trail condition, crowding, and an added cost per visit. Because each option carried a price, the choices people made reveal how much they are willing to pay for each feature — and that willingness to pay, in dollars, is the benefit they gain from the improvement.

The headline Result

People care more about trail quality than crowds.

For both residents and tourists, the quality of the surrounding habitat and the condition of the trail itself drive far more benefit than how crowded the path is. The benefit of a trail in good ecological health and well-maintained is worth roughly $63 per trip and $59 per trip respectively, over a degraded one.

Crowding matters — but less, and in opposite directions. Residents prefer quiet trails with fewer people. Visitors lean the other way: they value a moderately busy trail slightly more than an empty one, suggesting that a moderate activity may feel safer in an unfamiliar place.

What's the peferences on voluntary and supplemental funding mechanisms

The public supports additional funding for trails — but opinions on mechanism differ

Support for some form of voluntary and supplemental funding is broad: about 80% of residents and 90% of tourists support additional funding mechanisms to enhance trail management. Where they differ is in collection, methods, and cost sharing, and the two groups lean different ways.

Conclusion

The public benefit from improved trails is enormous. The current funding isn't reaching the trails.

Hawaiʻi already collects substantial visitor taxes, but that money largely flows into the State General Fund. The division that manages these trails sees no dedicated slice of it and runs on less than 1% of the state budget.

Our study shows that real investment is needed to match the value people place on public trails. In particular, prioritizing invasive species control and trail infrastructure generates the greatest benefit returns for both residents and visitors, a clear, evidence-based case for directing dedicated funding toward the improvements people value most.

Building on existing models like Hanauma Bay and Haʻena, the NAH program could adopt voluntary and supplemental funding system at high-use trailheads, designed around residents' preference for voluntary or per-entry contributions over mandatory fees, and the broad support for on-site and online collection this study documented. Framing the system around what residents actually prefer helps minimize the political risk of community pushback. Done well, it would give DOFAW a more stable, responsive funding stream and reduce dependence on state allocations that have historically fallen short, a growing concern as trail use rises and climate-driven flooding, erosion, and wildfire accelerate trail degradation.

How the math works

Benefit per trip comes straight from the study's willingness-to-pay estimates. Habitat and condition values are added together, then multiplied by your trip count.

Why crowding isn't here

Crowding carried the weakest, least consistent value — and for residents it wasn't statistically distinguishable from zero. We left it out of the stacking math to keep the estimate defensible.

A conservative floor

The default uses visitor values across an estimated 3.8M visitor trail-users. It excludes resident benefits entirely, so the real total is likely higher.
